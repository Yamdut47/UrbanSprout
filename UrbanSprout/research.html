Research

What I learned from five real websites

Good websites, like good gardens, are mostly about what you leave out. Before writing a line of code, I studied these five real sites — first as a visitor, then as a developer with the "view source" habit. All links open in a new tab so this page stays open.

The comparison

Five sites, side by side

Website What we share Where we differ What I improved on UrbanSprout
Nursery Live Online plant store, India Same product categories as UrbanSprout — seeds, planters and tools — each listed with a price and a discount. A huge inventory with search filters, customer accounts, live chat and a real payment gateway; pages are generated from a database. A curated eight-product catalogue in one readable table — original and discounted price visible side by side, no scrolling through dozens of near-identical cards.
Ugaoo Gardening brand & shop Combines a shop with a gardening blog, exactly like my Products and Blog pages, with strong plant-care content. Big promotional banners, pop-up offers and discount strips competing for attention on every screen. Zero pop-ups and zero banners. One palette, one pair of fonts, and a navbar and footer that stay identical on all five pages.
Amazon India Large e-commerce Product listings built from the same fields I show: name, original price, discounted price and a description. Accounts, sponsored ads, recommendation engines and real checkout; content is assembled by software, not hand-written. A hand-written static table that loads instantly, shows no ads, and whose "View Details" reveal is a few lines of plain JavaScript anyone can read.
Gardeners' World Gardening magazine site Long-form gardening articles split into clear sections, like my blog page. A large magazine with ads, newsletters, videos and dozens of parallel articles; built by a team on a CMS. Five short pieces on one theme with a clear start and a conclusion — a complete read in ten minutes and no distractions.
The Sill Boutique plant shop A curated, personal feel: few products, big photography and personality in the copy. Real e-commerce for the US — checkout, plant subscriptions and shipping tiers. The same boutique feel with honest Indian context: NPR pricing, balcony-sized advice, and a clear note that nothing here is actually sold.

Three things I took away

  • Prices only make sense when the original and the discount sit together — so every UrbanSprout product shows both, struck-through and bold, side by side.
  • A small, well-written site beats a big, noisy one for a first project — one palette, two fonts, five pages, zero pop-ups.
  • Consistent navigation is invisible when done right: the navbar and footer never change, so visitors stop thinking about where things are.
